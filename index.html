    Part 0

    The One Mental Shift That Makes Everything Click

    Before any mechanics, internalise one idea, because almost every confusion about payments dissolves once you have it.

    Key point
    Mastercard is not a bank. It never holds your money, never issues your card, and never decides whether your payment is approved. It is a network — it moves messages between banks, sets the rules everyone follows, and takes a small fee on the volume that flows across it.

    In your world at ZS, the platform (ZAIDYN) was where the value lived and where the money was. In payments, the bank on each side holds the money and takes the real financial risk, and the network in the middle is the neutral switch that connects them.

    From your world
    You already know a switch like this. When a patient fills a prescription, the pharmacy doesn’t call the insurer directly. It fires the claim into a clearinghouse (Change Healthcare, RelayHealth), which routes it to the right payer or PBM, gets back an approve-or-deny in seconds, and later reconciles the money. Mastercard is that clearinghouse, but for card payments. Hold this analogy — it carries through the whole guide.

    There is a second shift that matters specifically for your role. Your team does not run the network, and does not decide approvals either. Your team helps Mastercard’s customers — banks and processors — understand what the network did and why. You build the tools people use to look things up, spot problems, and decide what to fix.

    Key point
    Dhruba’s exact framing of the unit’s mission: data delivery, intelligence, and recommendation. Deliver the data, make sense of it, and tell the customer what to do about it. Everything your team builds should be doing one of those three things.
    Say it out loud
    In one sentence each: what does Mastercard do, what does it explicitly NOT do, and what does your specific team do that is different from both?

    Part 1

    How a Payment Actually Works

    Dhruba named this as your homework, in these words: how authorization happens, what authentication is, what 3-D Secure is, what clearing is, what settlement is. This part covers exactly that. It is the vocabulary everyone around you will use without explaining, and it is also the structure of the data inside your own product.

    1.1 The Four-Party Model

    Every card transaction has four players plus the network. These five roles are the grammar of the whole industry.

    PlayerWhat they are and what they do
    CardholderThe person buying. Holds the card, starts the payment.
    MerchantThe shop. Sells the goods, wants to get paid.
    IssuerThe cardholder’s bank. It issued the card, holds the money or the credit line, and takes the credit risk. The issuer is who actually approves or declines.
    AcquirerThe merchant’s bank. Processes transactions on the merchant’s behalf and deposits the money into the merchant’s account.
    Network (Mastercard)The switch in the middle. Routes the message from acquirer to issuer, carries the yes/no back, sets the rules, coordinates who owes whom. Holds no money, takes no credit risk.
    From your world
    Map it onto pharmacy claim adjudication: cardholder = patient; merchant = pharmacy; issuer = the payer or PBM (holds the funds, decides coverage, bears the cost); acquirer = the pharmacy’s claims processor; Mastercard = the switch routing the claim and enforcing the rules.
    Key point
    The issuer — not Mastercard — makes the approve/decline decision. This trips up everyone new. When your users ask ‘why was this declined?’, the answer almost always lies with the issuer, and your product’s job is to surface enough evidence to show why.

    One more role you will hear constantly: the processor

    A processor is the technology company that runs the plumbing on behalf of an issuer or acquirer. Most banks do not build their own card switches — they hire FIS, Fiserv, TSYS or similar. Processors are users of your product too, often the heaviest ones, because they operate on behalf of many banks at once.

    1.2 The Transaction Lifecycle — Four Events, Not One

    A single tap actually triggers several distinct phases that happen at different speeds and land in different databases. Confusing them is the most common rookie mistake — and understanding them is the core of your product, because Transaction Investigator’s headline feature is stitching them back together.

    Authentication — ‘is it really you?’

    This happens before or alongside authorization, and matters most for online purchases where no physical card is present. The standard is called 3-D Secure, usually shortened to 3DS. The old version (3DS1) was the clunky redirect page that killed conversion; it was retired around October 2022. The current version, EMV 3DS or 3DS2, passes over a hundred data points to the issuer so it can decide silently.

    • Frictionless flow — The issuer is satisfied by the data alone and the shopper sees nothing. This is the majority of cases and the goal.
    • Challenge flow — The issuer wants more proof and asks for a one-time passcode, a biometric, or an app confirmation.
    • Regulation — In Europe and the UK, Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) under PSD2 makes this legally mandatory for most online payments. In the US and India it is used more selectively.
    Key point
    The commercial point of 3DS is the liability shift. If a transaction was successfully authenticated and later turns out to be fraud, the loss moves from the merchant to the issuer. The trap: asking for an SCA exemption to reduce friction usually forfeits that protection. This is the kind of trade-off your users investigate after the fact.

    Authorization — ‘can this go ahead?’ (real time, under a second)

    The terminal or website sends an authorization request through the acquirer, across Mastercard’s network (called Banknet), to the issuer. The issuer checks the card is valid and active, that funds or credit are available, and that nothing looks wrong, then returns an approval or a decline code. An approval places a hold on the money. It does not move it.

    Three authorization variations worth knowing

    • Stand-in processing (STIP) — If the issuer is unreachable or too slow, Mastercard answers on the issuer’s behalf using limits the issuer pre-set, then tells the issuer later. When you see unexplained approvals the issuer has no record of, this is usually why.
    • Pre-authorization — A hotel or petrol station reserves an estimated amount before the final total is known. Incremental authorization adds to an existing hold, like a growing bar tab.
    • Reversals — Cancelling an authorization that will not be completed, releasing the hold. A missing reversal is a common source of ‘why is my money still held?’ complaints.

    Clearing — the paperwork (later that day, in a batch)

    Authorization only reserved the money. At the end of the day the merchant batches its approved transactions and sends them through the acquirer to Mastercard, which routes them to issuers to confirm the exact amounts. Mastercard’s clearing system is called GCMS (Global Clearing Management System) and the file format is called IPM. You will hear both names.

    Settlement — the money actually moves (usually T+1 or T+2)

    Mastercard calculates the net position of every bank against every other bank across the day’s transactions, and facilitates the money movement through a system called S.A.M. (Settlement Account Management). The issuer pays the acquirer minus interchange; the acquirer credits the merchant minus its own markup.

    From your world
    This is the same split you have seen in healthcare claims. The real-time approved/denied the pharmacy sees at the counter is authorization. The reimbursement dollars landing weeks later after remittance and reconciliation are clearing and settlement. An instant yes/no, then a slower money movement.
    Key point
    Authorization is a promise; settlement is the payment. A transaction can be authorized and later fail to settle. Keep the two ideas separate — and remember that your product’s core value is linking a transaction’s authentication, authorization and clearing records back into one story.

    1.3 The Economics — Who Takes a Cut

    When a shopper pays 100 units at a merchant, the merchant does not receive 100. It receives roughly 97.50. That gap is called the merchant discount rate, and it splits three ways.

    • Interchange — The largest slice (roughly 1.5–2%) goes to the issuer — compensation for taking the credit risk and issuing the card in the first place.
    • Scheme fee — A much smaller slice (roughly 0.1–0.3%) goes to Mastercard for routing, processing, and running the rules.
    • Acquirer markup — The remainder goes to the acquirer for processing on the merchant’s behalf.
    Key point
    Interchange is set by the network but paid by the merchant’s side and collected by the issuer. Mastercard does NOT keep interchange — only the small scheme fee. Mastercard’s revenue is a tiny toll on enormous volume.
    What this means for you
    Anything your product does that lifts approval rates or reduces disputes lowers your customers’ real costs. That is the business case template for almost everything you will propose.

    1.4 Card Mechanics and Idempotency

    TermWhat it is
    PANPrimary Account Number — the 16-digit card number. Your product lets users search by it.
    BIN / IINFirst 6–8 digits. Identifies the issuing bank, so the network knows where to route. Also how analytics get grouped by issuer.
    CVV / CVCThe 3–4 digit security code. Proves the card is physically held.
    EMV chipGenerates a one-time cryptogram per transaction, which is why chip fraud is far harder than magnetic stripe fraud.
    TokenizationReplacing the real card number with a device-and-merchant-specific substitute. Mastercard’s platform is MDES; it is what powers Apple Pay and Google Pay.

    Idempotency — the concept that prevents double charges

    If the same request arrives twice, the result should be the same as if it arrived once. A terminal sends a charge, the network hiccups, the terminal never sees a response and retries. Without idempotency you are charged twice. The request carries a unique key; if the system sees that key again it returns the original result rather than processing again.

    From your world
    Same problem on MCE with different stakes. If the orchestrator retries an agent run after a timeout, it should recognise it already generated that brief rather than regenerate a different one. Same input, same output.
    Say it out loud
    Ninety seconds, no notes: walk a card payment from tap to money landing in the merchant’s account. Name all four parties plus the network. Distinguish authentication, authorization, clearing and settlement, and say roughly when each happens. Then explain what stand-in processing is and why it confuses people.

    Part 2

    Inside the Transaction — the Payload Your AI Has to Explain

    This part did not exist in version 1, and it is now one of the most important. Dhruba described a core use case in almost exactly these words: a user does not know what a data element means, so they ask the bot, and the bot explains it — including how it relates to authorization, clearing and settlement. That means you need to understand the payload yourself.

    2.1 Why This Is a Product Problem, Not Just a Technical One

    Card messages use a format called ISO 8583. It is a numbered-field format: a message type, then a bitmap saying which fields are present, then the fields themselves, each identified only by a number. The critical property is that the message is not self-describing. It never says ‘this is the processing code’. It just says field 3, and both sides are expected to already know what field 3 means.

    Key point
    That single property is the reason your ‘explain this field’ feature has real value. A 35-year veteran knows DE39 by heart. A first-week analyst has no way to find out from the data itself. The knowledge lives in manuals, not in the message.
    From your world
    You have shipped against exactly this shape of problem. On MCE, the underlying data made sense to someone who knew the therapeutic area and the publication landscape, and was opaque to everyone else. The agent’s job was to close that gap in context, at the moment of need, rather than sending people to documentation.

    2.2 The Data Elements You Will Hear Most

    Mastercard calls these DEs (data elements); Visa calls them fields. You do not need to memorise these, but you should recognise them when a user or an engineer says them out loud.

    ElementWhat it means in plain terms
    DE2The card number (PAN).
    DE3Processing code — what kind of transaction this is (purchase, refund, cash advance).
    DE4The amount.
    DE11STAN — a trace number that matches a request to its response.
    DE22POS entry mode — how the card was read: chip, contactless, magnetic stripe, or keyed in. Hugely diagnostic when investigating declines.
    DE37Retrieval reference number.
    DE38The authorization code returned on approval.
    DE39The response code. 00 means approved; everything else explains the decline. The single most-investigated field in your product.
    DE41 / DE42Terminal ID and merchant ID.
    DE43Card acceptor name and location — the merchant descriptor a cardholder sees on a statement, and the usual cause of ‘I don’t recognise this charge’.
    DE55EMV chip data — the cryptogram and related chip fields.

    Message types and reference numbers

    • MTI (message type indicator) — 0100 authorization request, 0110 its response, 0120 an advice (used for stand-in), 0200 financial request, 0400 reversal request. Clearing uses its own IPM record types.
    • Banknet reference number — Mastercard’s network transaction ID, assigned at authorization. It is the key that links the authorization leg to later legs.
    • ARN — Acquirer Reference Number — traces a transaction through clearing, settlement and disputes. When someone asks you to ‘pull the ARN’, this is why.
    Key point
    Those two reference numbers are the spine of your product. They are how Transaction Investigator links authentication, authorization and clearing into one record — which is precisely the thing competitors cannot easily replicate.

    2.3 ISO 8583 vs ISO 20022

    ISO 8583 is the old standard: terse, rigid, numbered fields. ISO 20022 is the newer global standard: rich, structured, self-describing, far more data per message. The industry is migrating, slowly, and both will coexist for years.

    From your world
    The cleanest analogy: ISO 8583 is a telegram — minimal and rigid. ISO 20022 is an email with attachments — structured and extensible. If you want a healthcare parallel, it is roughly the leap from old fixed-format HL7 v2 messages to richer modern structured standards.
    What this means for you
    Richer messages mean better inputs for exactly the intelligence and recommendation work your unit exists to do. Migration also creates a translation problem between the two formats — and translation problems are explanation problems, which is your agent’s territory.
    Say it out loud
    Explain, without notes, why an AI that explains data fields is genuinely valuable in payments rather than a gimmick. Then name what DE39 is, what the ARN does, and why ISO 20022 matters to your product.

    Part 3

    When Things Go Wrong — Declines, Disputes and Chargebacks

    This is what your users spend their days on. Nobody opens a transaction research tool when everything worked. They open it because something was declined, disputed, or looks wrong.

    3.1 Declines — The Single Biggest Use Case

    A decline is a rejected authorization. The response code (DE39) says why, but codes are blunt and often deliberately vague, which is exactly why investigation tooling exists.

    Soft vs hard declines — the distinction that matters most

    • Soft decline — Temporary and retryable. The issuer was unreachable, a system glitched, funds were momentarily short. Retrying, sometimes at a better time or via a different route, often works.
    • Hard decline — Permanent. The card is expired, invalid, reported lost or stolen. Retrying is pointless and can trigger penalties.
    Key point
    Mastercard penalises excessive retries. Since November 2022, repeated failed attempts on the same card within 24 hours attract per-transaction fees. So ‘just retry it’ is not free advice — which makes intelligent retry recommendation a genuine product opportunity.

    Codes you will see constantly

    CodeMeaning
    00Approved.
    05Do not honour. The generic issuer decline — deliberately uninformative, and the single most frustrating code for investigators.
    14Invalid card number.
    51Insufficient funds.
    54Expired card.
    57Transaction not permitted to cardholder.
    59Suspected fraud.
    61 / 65Exceeds withdrawal amount limit / exceeds frequency limit.
    91Issuer unavailable — a soft, infrastructure-level decline.
    96System malfunction.
    From your world
    Code 05 is the payments equivalent of a claim rejection that just says ‘not covered’ with no further detail. You know something failed; you do not know what to change. Everything valuable your product does about declines is an attempt to turn code 05 into an actual explanation.
    What this means for you
    This is the clearest agentic AI opportunity in your product. Turning ‘code 05 on these 4,000 transactions’ into ‘these cluster around one BIN range during a two-hour window, consistent with an issuer-side outage’ is root-cause analysis, and it is exactly the ‘recommendation’ part of your unit’s mandate.

    3.2 Why False Declines Are Expensive

    A false decline is a legitimate transaction wrongly rejected. It costs the sale, and it damages trust in the card. Industry research cited in Mastercard’s own materials indicates that a large majority of customers reach for a different card or a different payment method after being declined, and a meaningful share abandon the purchase entirely. A further finding: a sizeable proportion of declines happen because of avoidable technical issues or policy errors rather than genuine risk decisions.

    Key point
    That last point is your product’s whole reason for existing. If a chunk of declines are avoidable mistakes rather than deliberate decisions, then finding and fixing them is pure recovered revenue for the customer — and that is a value proposition you can actually sell.

    3.3 Disputes and Chargebacks End to End

    A chargeback is a forced reversal of a transaction, initiated by the cardholder’s bank after a dispute. It costs the merchant the sale, often the goods, and a fee. Investigating them is a core use case for your product.

    Mastercard reason codes — four families

    FamilyCodes and what they mean
    Authorization4808 — the transaction was not properly authorized.
    Fraud4837 no cardholder authorization; 4863 cardholder does not recognise; 4870/4871 chip liability shift.
    Cardholder disputes4853 — the broad catch-all (goods not received, not as described, cancelled recurring); 4854.
    Point-of-interaction error4834 — duplicate charge, wrong amount, late presentment.

    Timelines matter operationally: fraud and cardholder-dispute codes generally carry 120-day windows (extending considerably for delayed delivery), authorization and point-of-interaction codes are tighter at around 90 days, and merchants typically get about 45 days to respond.

    From your world
    A chargeback is the payments version of a retroactive claim denial or clawback. The payer already reimbursed the pharmacy, then reverses it after an audit. Money that looked settled gets pulled back, and someone has to assemble evidence to fight it.

    What ‘researching a chargeback’ actually involves

    This is the workflow your product serves, step by step: pull the original authorization record; pull the matching clearing record; read the reason code; check whether 3DS authentication happened and whether liability shifted; find the linked reference numbers; look for related transactions from the same cardholder or merchant; assemble the evidence; decide whether to fight or accept.

    Key point
    Note the separation of tools: Transaction Investigator is where the evidence lives. Mastercom (a different Mastercard product) is where the dispute case is actually worked. Do not confuse them — yours supplies the truth, theirs runs the process.
    Say it out loud
    Explain the difference between a soft and hard decline and why it matters commercially. Then walk through what an analyst actually does to research a chargeback, naming the records they pull and in what order.

    Part 4

    Clearing, Settlement and Reconciliation — Your Sibling Team’s World

    Payment Reconciliation Intelligence sits alongside you under Operational Intelligence. You will not own it, but you will share stakeholders, data sources and probably architecture decisions — so understanding the shape of their problem is worth an hour.

    4.1 What Reconciliation Actually Means Here

    Reconciliation is the daily balancing of what a customer is told it owes or is owed against what the customer’s own books say. Mastercard sends each customer a daily net settlement advisement — the definitive statement of funds movement. The customer must tie that back to the detailed clearing records.

    • GCMS — Mastercard’s clearing system, which validates submitted transactions, calculates interchange, and computes each bank’s net position.
    • IPM — The clearing message format — the file layout acquirers submit and issuers receive.
    • S.A.M. — Settlement Account Management — the system that handles the actual money movement.
    • Breaks — Where the numbers do not tie out. Caused by rejected records, timing differences, currency conversion edits, or risk-management holds. Investigating breaks is the operational grind reconciliation tooling exists to reduce.
    From your world
    This is pharmacy remittance reconciliation. The pharmacy submitted claims, the payer paid a lump sum, and someone has to work out which claims that payment covered, which were short-paid, and why. The shape is identical: expected versus actual, and a queue of exceptions.
    What this means for you
    Reconciliation is a near-perfect agentic AI use case — high-volume, rules-based, exception-driven, with a clear right answer. If PRI has not started AI work either, there may be an argument for a shared agent layer across both teams rather than two separate builds. Worth raising once you understand the politics.

    Part 5

    Your Team, Your Products, Your Mandate

    This is the centre of the document. Everything before it was vocabulary; everything after it is what you do.

    5.1 Where You Sit

    Confirmed by your manager. Payment Performance Intelligence is your team; Reconciliation Intelligence is your sibling.

    Two further org facts to hold. First, you are in Global Product, not Regional Product. Global builds products that work everywhere; Regional takes them and adapts them for local conditions — Dhruba’s example was co-badging, common in Europe, rare in the US and India. Second, within Global Product you are in Product Development. You build the product; the Commercial organisation sells it; and there are further layers — Go-To-Market, Product Acceptance, and Implementation.

    From your world
    This maps almost exactly onto what you described to Dhruba on the call, and he agreed it was a similar model. At ZS, a product development team builds the core product, and delivery or PPS teams personalise it per client. Global Product is your PD team; Regional Product is the personalisation layer.
    Key point
    There is no onshore/offshore split. Dhruba was explicit: teams are distributed, with people in St. Louis, Mumbai, Pune and Europe, and senior leadership in the US because that is where the head office is. Do not carry an offshore-delivery mental model into this org — it will read as outdated.

    5.2 What Your Products Do

    Transaction Investigator — the research platform

    A searchable warehouse of transaction records, with deep historical data. Publicly it is marketed as Transaction Investigator Plus. Its documented capabilities are worth knowing precisely, because they define your differentiation:

    • Depth — Over 1,200 data points per transaction — framed by Mastercard as data only the network can provide.
    • Linkage — Complete transaction records including linked authentication, authorization and clearing. This is the crown jewel: the three legs stitched back into one story.
    • Search — Flexible search in bulk or by individual card number, and by theme — chargebacks, declines, or account holder.
    • Interpret and export — View guidance by use case, and export to CSV so customers can pull data into their own BI tools.
    • Access — Delivered through Mastercard Connect with no IT integration required. Low friction to adopt, which matters competitively.

    Dhruba described it as white-labelled among customer portals — which fits delivery inside Mastercard Connect and embedding within customer-facing experiences.

    The alerting product — real-time monitoring

    Publicly this appears to be Mastercard Early Detect Insights (MEDI). It monitors authorization performance in near-real time and alerts customers when something breaks — approval rates dropping, volumes behaving oddly, anomalies appearing. Thin historical data, unlike Transaction Investigator; the value is speed, not depth.

    Key point
    The compliance angle Dhruba described is real and specific. Mastercard Rule 3.4.1, the Authorization Data Insights Requirement, took effect 1 May 2024 for principal customers in selected EMEA countries. It requires near-real-time authorization monitoring — KPIs such as volume and approval rate with a data lag no greater than 30 minutes, plus real-time anomaly alerts. Customers can satisfy it three ways: build it, buy a third party, or take Mastercard’s product.
    What this means for you
    That three-way choice is the single most important commercial fact about this product. You are not selling into a vacuum; you are competing against build-it-yourself and against specialist vendors, with the mandate driving the purchase either way. It also means ‘prove we are compliant’ is a feature, not paperwork — audit trails and evidence may matter as much as the alerting itself.

    No AI work has started on this product yet. That is unusual latitude for a new joiner, and probably your clearest opportunity to shape something from zero.

    5.3 The Mandate, Restated

    Mission wordWhat it meansWhere it shows up
    Data deliveryGet customers the transaction data they need, reliably and in a usable formSearch, export, APIs
    IntelligenceMake sense of the data — patterns, anomalies, explanationsDecline analysis, anomaly detection, field explanation
    RecommendationTell the customer what to do about itRetry strategy, routing advice, config fixes — the least built-out and the biggest opportunity
    “We are not in the transaction business. We are not the ones who generate the transaction, we cannot improve the TPS. We are the ones who can improve the transaction in terms of — the transactions are getting declined, can we recommend something to the customer so they can take some action and that can improve their performance.” — Dhruba Chattaraj, on what the team does

    Draw your org from memory, four levels deep. Name both PPI products, what distinguishes them, and the compliance rule behind the alerting one. Then state the three-word mandate and give an example of each.

    Part 6

    Your Users and the Investigation Workflow

    Dhruba was clear that your product serves both internal Mastercard teams and external customers, and that the range of user sophistication is enormous. Understanding these people is most of the job.

    6.1 Who Actually Logs In

    PersonaWhat they are doing and what they need
    Issuer support / back-office agentA cardholder is on the phone disputing a charge or asking why they were declined. Needs one-call resolution: find the transaction, understand it, explain it in human language. Often not a payments expert.
    Acquirer / processor operationsWatching approval rates across merchant portfolios. Needs to spot a drop fast and find the cause.
    Chargeback / dispute analystWorking a case against a deadline. Needs evidence assembled, not raw data.
    Internal Mastercard customer technical servicesSupporting customers during incidents, using the same tooling.
    Strategy / decision makersDo not log in daily. Receive extracted reports that feed business decisions on portfolio performance.

    Dhruba also described a second kind of internal user: other Mastercard product teams who ask for new fields to be surfaced in your platform when they launch something. So you take requirements from inside the company as well as from customers.

    6.2 The Expertise Gradient — Your Central Design Problem

    “Somebody who’s been in payments for the last 35 years, and somebody who just started their career. Knowing every portion of that payload is necessarily important, but not necessarily present in every user.” — Dhruba Chattaraj

    Both use the same screen. The veteran wants raw fields fast and finds hand-holding insulting. The newcomer cannot even formulate the question. Traditional answers — role-based access, separate simplified views — fail because the difference is not permissions, it is knowledge.

    From your world
    You have argued this exact point before, in your own words on the call. On the ZS data subscription product, an associate, a consultant and a senior director all logged into the same application and needed to understand the same data differently. Role-based access could hide sections but could not explain anything. The answer was a layer on top that could infer, query and explain on demand. That is the same answer here.
    What this means for you
    This reframes what the AI is for. It is not a chatbot bolted onto a dashboard. It is the mechanism that lets one interface serve radically different expertise levels without dumbing down for the expert or abandoning the novice. That framing is also how you justify the investment internally.

    6.3 What an Investigation Actually Looks Like

    Walk this sequence until it is second nature, because every feature you build either speeds up a step or eliminates one:

    • A trigger arrives — a cardholder call, a monitoring alert, a merchant complaint, a batch of unexplained declines.
    • Search for the transaction by card number, reference number, or filter criteria.
    • Open the linked record — authentication, authorization and clearing together.
    • Read the codes: response code, reason code, entry mode, authentication result.
    • Form a hypothesis about root cause — issuer outage, configuration error, genuine risk decision, data problem.
    • Check whether the pattern repeats across other transactions.
    • Act: explain to the cardholder, fight the dispute, escalate to the issuer, or change a setting.
    Key point
    Steps four to six are where an agent earns its place. Reading codes, forming hypotheses and checking patterns are exactly what a well-grounded agent does faster than a human — and exactly where a wrong answer does damage, which is why evaluation matters.
    Say it out loud
    Name five personas and what each needs. Explain the expertise gradient problem to someone outside payments, and say why role-based access does not solve it. Then walk the seven investigation steps from memory.

    Part 7

    What You Actually Own as an Agentic AI PM

    On MCE, Chayan owned the agent building and the evaluation infrastructure; you owned product decisions on top. Here, with a title that says Agentic AI, more of the definition work sits with you. Dhruba described your role precisely: the AI specialists bring AI intelligence with no knowledge of the product; you bring the product knowledge; you marry them and you own the outcome.

    “You are coming into a very critical position where you have a left brain which speaks for AI, and then you need to have a right brain which is going to talk about — this is the product, this is how it behaves. I need to marry them.” — Dhruba Chattaraj

    7.3 Evaluation — The Capability That Separates a Real AI PM

    If you cannot clearly define what good means, you cannot safely ship. For agents specifically, evaluation must cover the whole loop rather than a single answer:

    • Tool correctness — Did it call the right tool, with the right inputs, at the right time?
    • Explanation accuracy — Did it explain a field or a code correctly, checked against the data dictionary?

    Agent Interoperability — When Their Agents Meet Yours

    You raised this on the call from your own experience with headless agents at ZS, and Dhruba confirmed it is live thinking inside Mastercard. Customers increasingly say: we have our own agents, can yours talk to ours?

    8.1 The Two Standards to Know

    • MCP — Model Context Protocol. The vertical standard — it connects a model or agent to tools and data. If you expose your product as an MCP server, a customer’s agent can call your platform as a tool. Now donated to the Linux Foundation with very wide adoption.
    • A2A — Agent2Agent. The horizontal standard — it lets agents from different vendors discover each other and delegate tasks between peers. Backed by Google and now also under the Linux Foundation.
    Key point
    The practical answer to ‘can your agent talk to ours?’ is: expose PPI via MCP so their agent can call yours as a tool, and plan for A2A so yours can work as a peer. Doing this early is cheap; retrofitting it is not.

    Pricing — the Thali Problem

    Dhruba raised this unprompted, which means it is on his mind. Today pricing is per-query or subscription. He wants to move to agent tiers, and used a thali analogy — different bundles at different price points, some agents more capable and more expensive than others.

    “This guy is fantastic in researching about declines, take it. The second one is also good in doing some projections for you. The third one, take the agent, it will do the customisation. These three agents will go, and based on customer need will charge.” — Dhruba Chattaraj, describing agent tiers

    10.1 Where the Market Actually Is

    Enterprise software is shifting away from per-seat pricing, which breaks when one agent replaces the work of many users. The emerging models:

    • Per outcome — Zendesk prices AI agents at roughly $1.50–$2.00 per automated resolution; Intercom’s Fin at about $0.99 per resolution; Salesforce Agentforce at around $2 per conversation.
    • Consumption — Pay for what you use. Predictable for the vendor, unpredictable for the CFO — which is why budget controls and per-agent cost visibility have become buying criteria.
    • Hybrid — A base subscription plus variable usage. The dominant transition model, and the most likely fit for a compliance-driven bank buyer.

    10.2 What This Means for Your Products

    Key point
    The thali framing is sound, but per-outcome pricing only works when the outcome is technically verifiable and cleanly attributable. ‘Resolved a decline investigation’ is measurable. ‘Provided intelligence’ is not. Start with tiers and metering; add outcome pricing only where you can prove the outcome.

    One more consideration specific to your alerting product: it is bought to satisfy a compliance mandate. Compliance buyers value predictability over cleverness. A pricing model that could spike unpredictably is a harder sell than a flat tier, even if the flat tier is more expensive on average.

    What this means for you
    This is a rare chance to form a view before you arrive. You could walk in with a one-page position on agent-tier pricing — what the tiers are, what each includes, where metering applies, and which outcomes are verifiable enough to charge for. Few new joiners bring a pricing point of view in month one.
